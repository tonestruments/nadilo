From Beat to Song

Good morning, class. Today we take one song apart and name every piece of it: the beat, the bar, the step, the two fours stacked on top of each other, and every other idea Nadilo School teaches without ever saying its name. You already know most of this. You have been nodding along to beats your whole life. All we are doing today is putting names on what you already feel.

Every on this page plays a sound, made right here in your browser. Laptop speakers work; headphones bring out the low notes. Nothing plays until you press something, and Esc stops whatever is playing.

Part 1

Time: notes, beats, bars and steps

What is a note?

Let's start with the word you will hear most. A note is one sound in a piece of music. Every note has three things:

  • when it starts,
  • how long it lasts,
  • and, most of the time, how high or low it sounds, its pitch. We'll come to pitch in Part 3.

People use the word in three different ways, and mixing them up causes most of the confusion:

  1. A sound you hear: "play that note again."
  2. A symbol on paper: the ovals with stems in sheet music.
  3. A name for a pitch: "that note is a C."

Drums play notes too. A drum hit has a start and a length; it just isn't tuned to a pitch you could sing. That's why drums are called unpitched instruments.

The partner of a note is a rest: a silence with a length. Rests are not empty space. They are part of the music, the way the gaps between words are part of a sentence.

The beat

Put on a song and your foot starts tapping. What your foot is tapping is the beat, also called the pulse: a steady row of moments, evenly spaced, that everyone playing the song agrees on.

Three things to know about the beat:

  • It is even. Every beat is the same distance from the next. That is what lets a band play together without watching each other.
  • It is a time, not a sound. Most beats have a drum on them, but a beat can be silent and you still feel it. Try it below: leave beat 3 empty and notice that your body still knows exactly where it is.
  • It is not the rhythm. The rhythm is the pattern of long and short notes and silences laid over the beat. The beat is the ruler; the rhythm is what you draw with it.

Tempo and BPM

Tempo is how fast the beats go. (It is the Italian word for "time".) We measure it in beats per minute, or BPM. At 60 BPM a beat comes once a second, like a clock's second hand. At 120 BPM, twice a second.

Nadilo's song runs at 88 BPM, so one beat lasts 60 ÷ 88 = 0.68 seconds. Drag the slider and watch every length change with it.

Before machines, composers described tempo in Italian words: adagio (slowly), andante (at a walking pace), allegro (literally "cheerful", so fast). In 1815 Johann Maelzel patented the metronome, a clockwork pendulum that ticks at a set speed, and composers could finally write an exact number. Beethoven was one of the first to use it. That is why old scores say things like M.M. = 88: Maelzel's Metronome, 88 beats per minute.

The bar

Listen to beats for a while and you start hearing them in groups. One beat in each group feels heavier, like the start of something: ONE two three four, ONE two three four. That group is a bar. (In American English it is also called a measure.) The heavy first beat is the downbeat.

In sheet music, bars are separated by vertical lines called bar lines, and that is where the name comes from.

Why group beats at all? Because grouping is how we keep our place. Counting to four, over and over, is far easier than counting to 112. Musicians say "from bar 9" the way you would say "from page 9".

Bars group too. A few bars, most often 4 or 8, make a phrase: a musical sentence that feels complete. A bar or a phrase that plays over and over is a loop.

The time signature: where 4/4 comes from

At the start of a piece of sheet music you will find two numbers stacked on top of each other. That is the time signature. It answers two questions:

44
  • The top number: how many beats are in a bar.Here, four.
  • The bottom number: what kind of note counts as one beat.Here, a quarter note.

So 4/4 means: four beats in every bar, and each beat is one quarter note long.

Why does 4 mean "a quarter note"? Because note lengths are named as fractions of one big note, the whole note. A half note lasts half as long, a quarter note a quarter as long, an eighth note an eighth. The bottom number is just the bottom of that fraction: 2 for halves, 4 for quarters, 8 for eighths. So 3/8 would mean three beats per bar, each one an eighth note.

And the slash? In sheet music there isn't one. The numbers are simply stacked, with no line between them. We write 4/4 because it is easy to type. It looks like a fraction, and it half behaves like one: a bar of 4/4 holds four quarter notes, which add up to one whole note. But it is not really a fraction. If it were, 3/4 and 6/8 would mean the same thing, since both equal three quarters. They don't. Listen to both.

The big C. Sometimes there is a where the numbers should be. Today it means 4/4 and we call it common time, but it is older than that name. In medieval notation, a full circle meant "divide into three", and musicians called that perfect time. A broken circle meant "divide into two". That broken circle is our C. A C with a line through it, , is cut time: 2/2, two big beats per bar.

Why is 4/4 everywhere? Four beats split evenly in half, and in half again, which suits walking, dancing and nodding your head. Most pop, rock, hip-hop and electronic music is in 4/4. Waltzes are in 3/4: ONE two three. The Dave Brubeck Quartet's Take Five is famous for being in 5/4.

How long is a note?

Here is the whole family of note lengths, called note values. Each one lasts exactly half as long as the one above it. That is the entire system. Press a row to hear a bar of it, against a steady click on every beat.

The British names are older and stranger. Breve is Latin for "short": centuries ago it was one of the shortest notes, then music slowed down around it, and today a breve lasts twice as long as a whole note. Minim comes from the Latin for "smallest", which it also once was. Crotchet is Old French for "little hook", after the note's old shape. Quaver comes from the old word for a trembling voice: quick notes, quivering.

Every note value has a matching rest, a silence of the same length. And three more things you will meet in sheet music, though Nadilo never needs them:

  • A dot after a note adds half its length again: a dotted quarter lasts one and a half beats.
  • A tie joins two notes into one longer note.
  • A triplet squeezes three notes into the time of two. Remember this one: it comes back when we get to swing.

Steps: the grid inside the bar

Now a word that doesn't come from sheet music at all. A step comes from the step sequencer: a drum machine that plays a pattern by stepping through a row of slots, one after another, and playing whatever is switched on in each slot. In 1980 Roland's TR-808 drum machine had a row of 16 buttons, one for each sixteenth note of a bar. Producers have pictured a bar as 16 steps ever since, and Nadilo stores every drum pattern the same way.

1 bar=4 beats=16 steps2.73 s · 0.68 s each · 0.17 s each, at 88 BPM

So why does a beat sometimes have one step, sometimes two, sometimes four? Because the beat never changes. Only how finely we slice it does. Think of a ruler: the distance from 0 to 1 centimetre is the same whether the ruler is marked in centimetres or in millimetres. Zooming in from 4 pads a bar to 8 to 16 doesn't make a beat any shorter. It draws more slots inside it.

  • 1 step per beat: quarter notes, 4 in a bar.
  • 2 steps per beat: eighth notes, 8 in a bar.
  • 4 steps per beat: sixteenth notes, 16 in a bar.

A hit on beat 2 is the same moment at every zoom. Try it below: change the zoom while it plays. Nothing you placed moves, and nothing you placed disappears. A note the coarser grid can't show is still there, drawn as a small dot between the slots.

Splitting a beat into smaller, equal pieces is called subdivision. Musicians count subdivisions out loud: 1 & 2 & ("one and two and") for eighths, and 1 e & a ("one ee and uh") for sixteenths.

The drums

The drum kit

A drum kit is a set of drums and cymbals that one person plays at once. Nadilo uses the three that matter most:

  • Kick, which Nadilo calls Boom. The biggest drum, played with a foot pedal, which is why it is also called the bass drum. Low and round: you feel it in your chest.
  • Snare, which Nadilo calls Crack. A drum with a row of metal wires, the snares, stretched under its bottom skin. When you hit it, the wires buzz and turn a thud into a crack.
  • Hi-hat, which Nadilo calls Tick. Two cymbals on a stand, clamped together by a pedal. Closed, they tick. Open, they hiss.

In Nadilo, as on most drum machines, the hi-hat lane sits on top, the snare in the middle and the kick at the bottom: high sounds up, low sounds down.

Building a groove, the way Nadilo does

A groove is a rhythm that makes you want to move. Nadilo's first world builds one in five moves. Press each one in turn below, and read along.

1 · Four on the floor

Put the kick on every beat and you get four on the floor: four kicks a bar, the pedal hitting the floor four times. It is the engine of disco, house and techno, and it is the first thing you build in Nadilo, in Heartbeat.

2 · The backbeat

Add the snare on beats 2 and 4. That is the backbeat. Beats 1 and 3 are the strong ones; 2 and 4 are the "back" beats, and putting the crack there is what makes heads nod. The kick says "here is the beat", and the snare answers "and here". Nearly every pop, rock and hip-hop song you know is built on it. In Nadilo, this is Clap back.

3 · Eighth notes on the hi-hat

Ticks between the beats keep time moving. A hi-hat on every eighth note, 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &, fills the gaps the kick and snare leave, so the groove never stands still. In Nadilo this is Zoom in, the level where the grid splits every beat in two.

4 · Ghost notes, velocity and accents

Real drummers never hit everything equally hard. A ghost note is a very soft hit, felt more than heard, usually on the snare and often just before a loud one. How hard a note is played is its velocity; the word comes from electronic keyboards, which measure how fast a key travels down. A note played louder than the ones around it carries an accent.

In Nadilo's Whisper hits, you tap a pad twice and it becomes a whisper, about 9 decibels softer than a full hit. The song's two whispers sit on the a just before each crack.

5 · Swing

Play the in-between notes perfectly evenly and a beat sounds like a machine. Push every second one a little late and it starts to swing: long-short, long-short, like skipping instead of walking.

Drum machines measure swing as a percentage. Roger Linn built swing into his LM-1 drum computer in 1979 and describes it like this: at 50% the two halves of each pair are equal, which is straight; at 66% the second one lands exactly where a triplet would put it, the classic swing feel. Settings in between just loosen a beat up.

Nadilo's swing works the same way, on the in-between sixteenths. Its slider runs from straight (50%) to a heavy 75%, and the song uses about 57.5%: a light lean you feel more than hear. One catch: swing only moves the in-between sixteenths, so you only hear it when there are notes there. That is why step 5 above fills the hi-hat with sixteenths.

Flam and feel: two details you hear but never see

  • When the kick and the snare land on the same step, Nadilo plays the snare 30 milliseconds late, so your ear hears two sounds instead of one blur. Drummers call two hits that close together a flam.
  • Nadilo also plays every snare 4 ms after the beat and every hi-hat 9 ms after it. That tiny lateness, playing just behind the beat, makes a groove feel relaxed, or laid back. How a groove sits against the beat is its feel.

Samples, one-shots and kits

Where do the sounds come from? A sample is a recording of a sound. A one-shot is a sample that plays once, all the way through, every time it is triggered: a single drum hit, a single pluck. A sample of a whole repeating pattern is a loop. A kit is the set of sounds you choose for your lanes.

Nadilo's sounds are real recordings from Cymatics sample packs. On this page everything is synthesised by your browser instead, so it sounds plainer than the app.

Pitch: high and low

High and low

Pitch is how high or low a note sounds. A sound is air vibrating, and pitch is how fast it vibrates, measured in hertz (Hz): vibrations per second. The A that orchestras tune to vibrates 440 times a second. Low notes vibrate slowly; high notes vibrate fast.

Musicians name pitches with the letters A B C D E F G, then start again. Between most neighbouring letters sits one more note, named with a sharp (♯, a little higher) or a flat (♭, a little lower). On a piano, those are the black keys. That makes 12 different notes before the pattern repeats, and the distance from one to the next is a semitone, or half step.

When the pattern repeats, the note has the same letter, an octave higher. It vibrates exactly twice as fast and sounds like the same note, only higher. Middle C vibrates about 262 times a second; the C above it, about 523.

Scales, keys and home

A song doesn't use all 12 notes equally. It mostly uses a handful that sound good together: a scale. The scale plus its home note is the song's key. The home note is the tonic: the note everything wants to come back to.

Keys come in two main flavours. Major sounds bright. Minor sounds darker, more serious or more wistful. Nadilo's song is in C minor.

A pentatonic scale has only five notes (penta is Greek for five). The C minor pentatonic scale is C, E♭, F, G and B♭. Almost any order of them sounds good together, which is why it is the first scale many guitarists learn for solos, and why Nadilo uses it for the tune.

Nadilo's two ladders

Nadilo never shows a note name. Instead it gives you two ladders, six heights each, chosen from the key so that nothing you place can sound wrong. A bigger dot is a deeper note; the ringed dot is home, C. Here is what is behind each rung.

  • Laptop speakers can't really play below about 55 Hz, so the bass ladder starts at C2, 65 Hz.
  • One recording, many notes. Nadilo plays every bass note from a single recording of a bass guitar playing C2. To play a higher note, it plays the recording faster: each semitone up is 1.0595 times faster (the twelfth root of two), so twelve semitones up is exactly twice as fast, an octave. The tune works the same way from one kalimba note, C5. A kalimba is a thumb piano: metal tines plucked with the thumbs.

Harmony: chords and moods

Chords

A chord is three or more notes played at the same time. The basic chord is a triad: three notes stacked with one scale note skipped between each, called the root, the third and the fifth. The root is the note the chord is named after.

The difference between a major chord and a minor chord is a single note: the third. Lower it by one semitone and bright turns dark. Listen:

The notes of whichever chord is playing are its chord tones. A bass or tune note that is a chord tone fits. A note that isn't can clash; musicians call it dissonant. Clashes aren't mistakes, they are tension, and good music uses them on purpose. But a beginner's bass note that clashes usually just sounds sour, which is why Nadilo shakes it.

Chord progressions

A chord progression is the order the chords come in. Nadilo's song repeats four, one per bar: home, warm, bright, lift. That is C minor, A♭ major, E♭ major and B♭ major, or i–VI–III–VII.

If those four sound familiar, they should. C minor has a relative major, E♭ major, which uses exactly the same notes. Seen from E♭, the same four chords read vi–IV–I–V, one of the most used progressions in pop. The Cranberries' Zombie is built on it.

New wordschord progressionrelative major

Coming home

Play a progression and stop. If it stopped on the home chord, it sounds finished. If it stopped anywhere else, it sounds like a question, or a sentence cut off. Moving from tension back to rest is called resolving, and the way a phrase ends is its cadence.

The bass line

The bass line is the lowest part of a song. Most of the time it plays the root of each chord, which is what makes a chord sound solid. Nadilo teaches two habits, in this order:

  • Lock to the kick (Follow the boom). Play bass notes where the kick drum hits and the two sound like one big instrument.
  • Follow the chords (Moods move). When the chord changes, the bass moves to one of its chord tones. The song's bass plays each bar's root on beats 1 and 3, then steps up to another chord tone on beat 4 where there is room, leaning into the next bar.

A bass that plays between the beats, on the &, is syncopated. Syncopation means putting weight where the beat doesn't expect it. Nadilo's ladder has one cell per beat for now, so syncopation is the next idea rather than this one.

Part 5 · Nadilo's fourth world, Voice

Melody

The tune

A melody, or tune, is a line of single notes, one after another: the part you hum. The span from its lowest note to its highest is its range.

Melodies are built from phrases, short musical sentences. Very often one phrase asks and the next one answers; musicians call that call and response. Nadilo's tune has four one-bar phrases: a phrase, an answer, the phrase again, and a landing.

Silence matters as much as notes. The rests between phrases are where a listener breathes.

Motif and landing

A motif is a short idea that comes back, like the four notes that open Beethoven's Fifth Symphony. Repeating it is what makes a tune catchy. In Nadilo's tune, bar 3 repeats bar 1 exactly.

Ending on the tonic, the home note, makes a melody sound finished, the same way a progression does when it comes home. Nadilo's tune lands on C in bar 4.

Part 6 · Nadilo's last world, Story

The whole song

Sections

A song is made of sections, each with its own job:

  • Intro: sets the scene, often with just a few parts.
  • Verse: moves the story along. In a song with words, the lyrics change each time.
  • Chorus: the big, repeated part, usually the most memorable. Its catchiest bit is the hook.
  • Bridge: a contrasting section, usually heard once, often before the last chorus. Nadilo's song doesn't have one.
  • Break, or breakdown: parts drop out, usually the drums, so their return hits harder. In dance music that return is the drop.
  • Outro: the ending. Parts leave one by one.
New wordssectionintroversechorushookbridgebreakdowndropoutro

Arrangement

Which parts play in which section is the arrangement. Adding parts raises the energy, a build-up; taking them away lowers it. How many parts play at once is the song's texture. Here is Nadilo's whole song: 28 bars, about one minute sixteen seconds. Press a section to jump to it.

Count the parts in each section: 2, 4, 3, 4, 2. The line rises, dips, peaks and falls. That shape is the story. A flat line is just a loop.

Words from the studio

Nadilo avoids these, but you will hear them the moment you talk to anyone who makes music on a computer.

Track
A song in progress. In music software it also means one lane of it: the drum track, the bass track.
Part
One instrument's line in a song: the drums, the bass, the chords, the melody.
Layer
A part stacked on top of others. Layering is adding parts one at a time.
Pattern
A short rhythm or figure, usually one to four bars, that repeats.
DAW
Digital Audio Workstation: software for making music, like GarageBand, Ableton Live, FL Studio or Logic Pro.
Piano roll
A grid in music software with time running across and pitch going up, where each note is drawn as a bar.
MIDI
Musical Instrument Digital Interface (1983): a language instruments and computers use to send notes to each other. It says which note, how hard and when; it carries no sound itself.
Quantize
Snapping notes to the nearest step of the grid, so they land exactly in time.
Playhead
The line or light that shows where playback is. In Nadilo it is the glowing column.
Mix
How loud each part is against the others, and the work of balancing them.
Stems
A finished song split into a few files, one per part or group: drums, bass, chords, vocals.
Latency
The delay between pressing something and hearing it. Bluetooth headphones add a lot; Nadilo's Sync screen measures it and moves the lights to match.
Sample pack
A collection of samples made for producers to use in their own songs.
