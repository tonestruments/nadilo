1 · The short answer

Their bar, then yours

Today the game is a quiz. You listen, click cells, press Check and read which drum is off, and nothing on the screen teaches the listening. The coach line even asks you to clap along, but nothing hears the clap. Six changes turn the quiz into a lesson, and the screen gets quieter while they do it.

Two bars, one loop.

The hidden beat plays a bar, then your bar answers it. When the loop sounds like one unbroken groove, you have it.

Tap along.

F is the kick, J the snare, K the hi-hat. A tap lands on the nearest step, so your body finds the beat and the grid writes it down. Clicking still works, for fixing.

The loop is the judge.

The first time your bar plays exactly like the hidden one, the curtain lifts at the next bar line. Check is still there when you want to know which drum is off.

The reward happens on the grid.

No card covers the music. The hidden notes appear where they always were and light up as they play, and the task turns into the beat’s name.

The grid splits when the music does.

Four pads a bar for the first three levels, eight from Stomp Stomp Clap, sixteen from The Push. Half the wrong places to look while you are still learning to listen.

Every beat can drive a song.

Once a level is solved, In a song plays Nadilo’s bass, chords and tune over it, live. Same song, new beat, new feel: the first real lesson in how a track is built.

2 · The gap

What other apps leave out

Producers do one thing with drums all day. They hear a beat, in a record or in their head, and put it on a grid. Every tool out there teaches one half of that move.

Copy a beat you love and you have learned it. Tap out the one in your head and you have written it. It is the same move: Hidden Beats practises the first, so the second comes free.

3 · The approach

Hear it, tap it, see it, match it, name it, use it

This is how musicians have always learned a groove, in the order a good teacher uses. Each step is one thing on the screen, and none of them needs a paragraph.

  1. Hear it.

    The hidden bar plays whole, under a curtain. You never see what you are listening for. Kodály, Orff and Suzuki all start here: sound before symbol.

  2. Tap it.

    Your hands already know where the snare is. Most people can clap on 2 and 4 at a concert without counting. Émile Jaques-Dalcroze built a whole method on that: rhythm is learned through the body before it is written down.

  3. See it.

    Each tap snaps to the nearest step, the way a drum machine records. The grid is notation without the theory: no staff and no note values, only where.

  4. Match it.

    Their bar, then yours, is echo clapping: the teacher claps, the class claps it back on the same pulse. It is also call and response, which runs from West African music through gospel, blues, funk and hip hop. Two bars back to back make a one-step difference easy to hear.

  5. Name it.

    Only when the bars match does the beat get its name. The name is the reward, never the homework, as VISION.md already promises.

  6. Use it.

    Then hear it drive a song. Maria Witek and colleagues found that beats with a medium amount of syncopation make people want to move the most. The thirteen climb that hill, from the plain backbeat to the off-centre kicks of level 13.

4 · Why two bars

Hear the difference

One wrong answer, heard two ways. The hidden snare is on 2 and 4. This attempt put the first one half a beat late. Press each, and listen for the late snare.

Today the player hears the hidden beat twice, reaches for another button, and hears their own twice. Memory has to hold the first while the second plays, across a restart. In call and response the two sit side by side on one unbroken pulse, so the late snare comes out as a hiccup every other bar. The ear does the comparing, and the grid only has to show where.

It also changes what Check is for. The loop tells you whether you are right; Check tells you which drum is not.

5 · Why tapping

Tap, and it lands

A tap does not have to be perfect. It lands on the nearest step, so anything within half a step counts.

At 96 BPM, half a step is 312 ms either side with four pads a bar, 156 ms with eight and 78 ms with sixteen. People tap a few tens of milliseconds ahead of a beat they can predict (Repp, 2005), well inside every window.

The tap sounds the moment you hit it, so you hear your own timing. The note lands on its step, and next time round it plays exactly there. A producer would call that quantising. Here it is simply what a tap does.

Taps go through the same offset the Sync screen measures, so Bluetooth headphones do not push every tap a step late. A tap only ever adds a note. Taking one away is a click, or Undo.

7 · The thirteen

The same thirteen beats, re-planned

The patterns stay. They are the right thirteen, from the backbeat to syncopation, and their given notes fade the way the music teacher suggested. Three things change. The screen shows what is given, so no line has to say it. The grid zooms in two steps. And each level ends on one sentence that says what happened in plain words before it names it.

8 · The look

A curtain, two bars, three keys

  1. The hidden bar is a curtain. Indigo with a faint wave, the seigaiha already along the bottom of every Nadilo screen. It looks the same in both themes. Only the given notes and the playhead show on it, and nothing on it ever moves with a hidden note.
  2. Your bar is the grid you know. Flat slots in three shades, solid notes, striped given notes, exactly as today.
  3. A tap leaves a ring on the curtain. It marks where you tapped, never where you should have.
  4. The pads are the drums. A name and its key. Pressed, the key fills with the drum’s colour. No captions: press it and hear it.
  5. Whose bar is playing is ink. The label over the playing bar turns ink and the other stays muted. Nothing else on screen says whose turn it is.
  6. The stamp lands beside the name. Heard, with how it went: By ear, First check, 3 checks. It never covers a note.

9 · Home

Thirteen curtains

Home keeps its cards. An unsolved level is a closed curtain with its task. A solved one shows its beat, its name and how it went, and By ear joins First check, 3 checks and Answer shown. These cards follow what you solve in the prototype at the top of the page.

10 · From today’s app

What stays, what changes

Stays

  • The thirteen patterns, their order and their given notes.
  • Hidden notes never in the DOM, and never flashing as they play. Every note you can see fires when the playhead reaches it, right or wrong.
  • Check names the drum, never the step. Show me after two checks.
  • Next goes to the next unsolved level. Solving celebrates once, and the first solve is recorded.
  • The flat grid, the pills, the 60 px play button. Pads and cells play on press.
  • 96 BPM, the feel offsets and the flam on a stacked snare.

Changes

  • Grid.tsx draws two bars, Hidden beat and Yours. levels.ts gains res: 4 | 8 | 16.
  • drumProgram becomes one 32-step loop, the target and then the given and placed notes, read live so edits, Slow and In a song land on the next step.
  • F, J, K and the pads send TAP. A pure snapTap() in logic.ts turns the moment heard into a step, using Sync’s offset.
  • A pass of Yours that matches, with no edit during it, sends HEARD: the curtain lifts at the next bar line. Check still works and still counts.
  • RewardDialog leaves the ear game. The head takes the name and one named line, which replaces listenFor and heard; the stamp moves beside the name.
  • New: Slow (96 to 64 BPM) and In a song (solved levels). Gone: Coach tips with their switch, the lane captions, Play yours.
  • Home draws unsolved tiles as curtains, and By ear joins the outcomes.

Tests follow the same rules as today: levels.test.ts also checks that every target fits its grid, game.spec.ts keeps its hidden-note checks for both bars, and the size checks that keep School still apply to the ear game. The demo film shows 0.2.0 and does not change.

11 · Before building it

Risks, and the first test

Can a beginner tap in time?Clapping on 2 and 4 is easy; a kick a sixteenth before beat 2 is not. Slow helps, and clicking still works. Watch how many notes are tapped and how many are clicked.
Half as much hidden beat.The hidden bar now plays every other bar. If first-timers ask to hear it again and again, let a press on the curtain loop it alone.
A curtain that lifts by itself.It lifts only after a full pass of your bar matches, so it always lifts on a bar line, as the hidden notes start to play. If it reads as a glitch, make Check the only way.
Bluetooth headphones.Without a Sync offset every tap lands late. If most taps land a step after the player’s own notes, point at Sync.
Thirty-two steps across.At sixteen pads a bar in an 1180 px window a step is about 24 px wide. Taps do the placing and clicks the fixing, and School already draws 64 steps across.
Will people find F, J and K?The key is printed on each pad, and it glows once if a loop passes without a tap. If that is not enough, level 1’s line can say “Tap J with the snare”.

The test

Five beginners, no instructions, twenty minutes each, on levels 1, 4, 6, 10 and 13.

  • Time to the first tap, and whether it was a key, a pad or a click.
  • How many notes are tapped and how many clicked, and how far off the taps are.
  • Checks per level, and how many levels are solved with no check at all.
  • Whether anyone presses In a song, and whether they play it twice.
  • Afterwards, in their own words: what is the backbeat? What changed in half time? What did the dark bar mean?
