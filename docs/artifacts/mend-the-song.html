Mend the Song

You asked the question the whole School turns on. When someone hears a song and then rebuilds it, step by step, what should they hear at each step: just that part, the song up to that part, the whole song, or different versions in layers? Here is my answer, with sound you can play, and a look for School that doesn't borrow from any DAW.

1 · The short answer

Never take the song away

The song stays whole.

Every level plays inside the whole song. What changes from level to level is who plays each part: one by one, your parts replace the song's own.

Three ways to listen, over the same four bars.

The song (why this part matters), Up close (the part with the one part it talks to) and Your turn (the song with a hole where this part was, filled by your notes). Switching never stops the music.

Done is when Your turn sounds like The song.

The ear is the check. Stars say how close you came; the goal stays lenient, as it is today.

The whole song, three times.

When you meet it; at the end of every world, with every part you have rebuilt so far; and at the very end, when every part is yours.

2 · Listen before deciding

Five ways to preview a part

Here are the four options you listed, and one more, playing Nadilo's own song. Pick a level, press any option, and switch between them while it plays: the music never restarts, so you hear each difference at the same moment of the bar. Then read what I would do with each one.

Because Nadilo plays its song from notes, not from a recording, every one of these mixes is free: no stems to export, no extra audio files to ship.

3 · The model

You mend the song, row by row

Think of the song as a piece of cloth with every part stitched into it. When you start, every stitch is the song's own. Each world unpicks one row; you stitch it back, level by level, and from then on that row is yours. The song never stops being whole. It just becomes more and more yours, until every stitch is.

The rows you haven't reached yet are basted in: held by long, loose, temporary stitches, the way a tailor holds a garment together before sewing it for real. You hear those parts, because the song needs them, but you don't see their notes until you rebuild them. Nothing is given away.

There is an old riddle about a ship whose planks are replaced one at a time, until none of the originals are left: is it still the same ship? That is Nadilo School. At the end, the learner owns a song they first heard as someone else's, and they know every stitch in it.

The cumulative version, only what you have made so far, doesn't disappear. It becomes Only mine, one press away in the song strip at the bottom of every level. It is how a producer hears a song grow; it just isn't the right thing to learn from.

4 · Why this works

Teachers have always done it this way

  • It is how a teacher runs a lesson. "Listen to the record. Now just the bass and the drums. Now play along with the record." Play-along records with one part left out have existed since 1950, when Irv Kratka started Music Minus One with a Schubert quintet missing its piano. Karaoke is the same idea for singers.
  • Popular musicians learn by copying whole recordings. Lucy Green's research, and the Musical Futures classroom programme built on it, found that popular musicians learn by ear from real recordings, not from exercises.
  • Sound comes before symbol. Kodály, Orff and Suzuki all start from listening and singing, and Edwin Gordon's Music Learning Theory names the goal: audiation, hearing music in your head. Nadilo already names things only after you have done them.
  • Hearing one part inside a mix is a learned skill. Picking one sound out of several at once is what Albert Bregman called auditory scene analysis, and musicians measurably do it better than non-musicians (Zendel and Alain, 2009). Up close isolates the part; The song puts it back; going between the two is the practice.
  • Support should fade. Learning research calls it fading: a worked example first, then a half-finished problem, then a problem on your own. Nadilo's traced levels, carry-it-on levels and by-ear levels are exactly that, and the needle holes are the worked example.
  • Fun is learning a pattern. Raph Koster's point in A Theory of Fun: we enjoy what we are in the middle of understanding. Hearing the whole first, then its parts, then the whole again, is what he calls chunking.
5 · One level, five moves

Clap back, rebuilt in cloth

  1. Hear it in the song. The level opens on The song, always the same four bars.
  2. Up close. The part with its partner. After one pass, Up close glows; nothing switches on its own.
  3. Your turn. The song plays with a hole. Touch the cloth and it becomes your turn: every stitch sounds where it lands.
  4. It clicks. When the goal is met, the row's seams sew themselves shut, and only then does the part get its real name.
  5. Make it yours. Takes, and your own changes, get gold patches.

Try it. The Boom row is yours, from Heartbeat. The Tick row is basted: you'll hear it, but its notes stay hidden until Zoom in. The Crack row is empty, with needle holes where the song's cracks go in bar 1. Tap the cloth to stitch.

7 · A look that isn't a DAW

Cloth and thread

Nadilo's icon is already a kinari logomark on an indigo tile, and its screens already carry a seigaiha wave along the bottom. Indigo cloth with white running stitches is sashiko, a Japanese way of mending cloth: a grid of small marks, repeated. That is exactly what a rhythm looks like. So School is drawn as cloth and thread, and every musical idea gets a physical mark you could sew.

The rules

  1. The metaphor lives in the pictures, never in the words. The screen never says stitch, thread or sashiko. It says Boom, Bar 2, Your turn.
  2. Nothing is a box. Empty time is just cloth. The warp threads show the steps; the seams show the bars.
  3. Every drum has its own stitch, so colour is never the only clue: a solid satin stitch for Boom, a cross for Crack, a slanted seed stitch for Tick.
  4. Length is length. A bass or tune note is a running stitch as long as the note, with a knot where it starts. Height is pitch. A thinner stitch is softer.
  5. One thing moves: the needle. Stitches catch the light as it passes, on the frame they are heard.
  6. You only see what you have rebuilt. Everything else is basted.
  7. Gold means yours. Anything you change after rebuilding gets a gold patch behind it, so the finished cloth shows exactly how you made the song your own.

8 · The screens

From meeting the song to owning it

Meeting the song

The song gets a screen of its own and a real player, as you asked: play, pause, and press anywhere on the ribbon to jump. As it plays, a running stitch follows the needle, so the time bar is made of the same material as everything else. The ribbon shows how long the song is and where you are, and nothing about how it is built.

Make it yours

Once a part is rebuilt, the sparring partner offers takes. A take is pinned on with dashed gold outlines, the stitches it would remove left as needle holes, and it plays straight away. Sew it on to keep it: its new stitches get gold patches, and keep them, so by the end the cloth shows, at a glance, everywhere the learner made the song their own.

The end of a world

When a world is done, the whole song plays from the top, with your rows in it. Your row stitches itself in as the needle passes, wherever it actually plays, so you discover the song's shape by hearing it. The rows you haven't rebuilt stay basted.

9 · From today's app

What stays, what changes

Stays

  • Rebuild the song, then make it yours; tracing, then carrying on, then by ear.
  • Stars, the lenient goal, best stars kept for good.
  • Do it, hear it, then name it. No paragraphs on screen.
  • Four bars on every instrument, with the drum bar shown four times.
  • The song's own screen, with play, pause and a time bar.
  • The sparring partner's takes; ear checks as A and B cards.
  • Instruments play on press; nothing appears on its own; centred cards.

Changes

  • The song and Play yours become three positions: The song, Up close, Your turn.
  • Your track becomes the song strip: the whole song as it stands, with Only mine one press away.
  • Pads and ladder cells become stitches on cloth, each drum with its own stitch.
  • Breathing dashed outlines become still needle holes, so nothing on screen moves except the needle.
  • The Playing yours tab goes: the chosen position always says what is playing.
  • Every world ends with the whole song, your rows in it.
  • Home becomes the cloth. Each level gains one piece of data: its Up close partner.
10 · Before building it

Risks, and the first test

Will people tap cloth that has no boxes on it?Watch the first tap: how long it takes, and where. Needle holes and the hover pin are the answer if it works; faint slot marks are the fallback.
Will anyone leave The song?Count the switches to Up close and Your turn. If nobody switches, step through the three positions automatically the first time, once.
Is hearing what you can't see confusing?Listen for "where is that sound coming from?" If it comes up, light the basted row in the song strip while it plays.
Can a beginner hear Your turn against The song?This is the skill itself. Watch the three-star rate in the by-ear levels; if it is near zero, Up close needs to come back in those levels.
Sixteen stitches a beat-bar is tight.At sixteen per bar, stitches are short. Check legibility at the smallest window, 1180 × 760.

The test

Five beginners, no instructions, twenty minutes each: meet the song, then Heartbeat, Clap back, Zoom in and Follow the boom, on a paper-thin prototype like the one above.

  • Time to first stitch, and where it lands.
  • Which listening positions they use, and in what order.
  • Stars in each level, and whether anyone stays for three.
  • Afterwards, in their own words: what is a bar? what did the snare do? whose music was playing?
  • One question about the look: "What does this remind you of?" If the answer is "a DAW", it failed.
